Lightning Around the Clock

Where lightning strikes is only half the story. Drag the clock: storms over land build through the afternoon and fade at night, but some spots over water, like Lake Maracaibo (the place with the most lightning on Earth), peak after dark instead. Click any location to see its hour-by-hour profile.

Write-up

Design rationale

Our first map showed where lightning was concentrated in the August 2023 GOES-16 sample. We wanted to know when those places were active and which ones broke the usual afternoon pattern. In the pinned profiles, Central Florida peaks between 2 and 3 p.m. local solar time, while Lake Maracaibo and Kansas both peak between 2 and 3 a.m. About 86% of the flashes around the lake fell between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m., compared with 12% around Central Florida.

We tried a map of each cell’s busiest hour and a set of four snapshots. The peak-hour map reduced each place to one value and hid the rest of its daily pattern. The snapshots made the change visible at four points in the day. The slider keeps that comparison on one map and lets you stop at any of the 24 local solar hours. Converting from UTC to local solar time makes it possible to compare afternoon activity across longitudes.

Location sets the position on the map. The color runs from dark purple to bright yellow as the flash rate rises. We use the same logarithmic scale throughout the day so weaker activity stays visible alongside dense hotspots. The rates account for observation time and area, and pooling neighboring cells makes sparse hourly counts less noisy. The profiles show each hour as a share of that location’s flashes, so a quieter place can still be compared with an active one. A pin keeps its color in the map and chart, and the land and water lines give each location a reference. Clicking the chart changes the map hour, so you can find a peak in the profile and immediately see where else lightning is active at that hour.

Development process

For the checkpoint, we sampled satellite observations from one month and used six static plots to compare when lightning peaked across the map. The final site loads the binned counts and draws each hour and each pinned profile in the browser.

Kylan Huynh came up with the initial idea. Sebastian Roberson helped write the code for the interactive site. Kevin Pyo made visualizations for the project and recorded the checkpoint video.

We spent about 13 person-hours on the project. We estimate most of the development time went into preparing the GOES-16 data and linking the map to the hourly profiles. We grouped flashes by location and local solar hour, then built the controls that let viewers change hours and compare places across both views.
